Zakat on Stocks: How to Calculate It Correctly (2026)

ByZakariya Shafi October 13, 2026October 16, 2026

Short answer: Pay 2.5% of the current market value of your zakatable stocks once a lunar year passes. Dividends already received count toward your cash; purification of impure income is a separate obligation.

Are Stocks Zakatable? The Fiqh Reasoning

Yes — by overwhelming scholarly consensus, stocks held as investments are treated like trade goods (urud al-tijarah). The reasoning has three layers:

  1. Intent: You bought them to grow wealth — the defining feature of trade goods in classical fiqh.
  2. Liquidity: They have a clear, real-time market value and can be sold in seconds — unlike, say, your home.
  3. Scholarly consensus: From the Fiqh Council of North America to Darul Uloom Karachi to AAOIFI scholars, the ruling is settled: investment stocks are zakatable annually.

This applies whether you hold individual stocks, halal ETFs like SPUS/HLAL, or mutual funds like Amana.

Individual Stocks vs ETFs vs Mutual Funds

The principle is the same, but the mechanics differ slightly:

The Calculation (3 Steps)

Step 1: On your zakat date, note the market value of all shares (not what you paid — what they’re worth today).

Step 2: Add any dividends received and not yet spent to your cash total.

Step 3: Pay 2.5% of the combined stock value.

Worked Example: A Realistic Portfolio

Dividends: Received vs Reinvested (DRIP)

  • Cash dividends sitting in your account: Add to your zakatable cash total (as in the example above).
  • Dividends auto-reinvested (DRIP): They bought more shares — so they’re already inside your market value. Don’t count twice.
  • Dividends already spent: Gone — not zakatable.

Don’t Confuse Zakat with Purification

These are two separate obligations — this is the #1 confusion I see:

  • Zakat (2.5%) — on the market value, paid to eligible zakat recipients
  • Purification — donate the impure portion of dividends/gains (impure income ÷ total revenue) as sadaqah with no reward intended, to any charitable cause

Purification Worked Example

You received $500 in dividends from a company whose financials show 4% impure (interest-based) income:

  • Purify: $500 × 4% = $20 → donate as sadaqah (no reward intended)
  • Zakat: Pay 2.5% on your total stock market value as normal

The $20 purification does NOT reduce your zakatable base — they’re parallel obligations. (See our halal investing guide for the AAOIFI screening behind this.)

Special Cases

Stocks at a loss: Still zakatable on current market value — losses don’t cancel the obligation if you’re above nisab overall. A $10,000 investment now worth $6,000 owes zakat on $6,000.

401(k) stocks: Covered in our zakat on 401(k) guide — the Fiqh Council of North America holds zakat is due annually on the vested balance.

Day traders: If you trade frequently, some scholars treat each holding period separately; most apply the standard annual calculation on your zakat date. Keep records — your brokerage annual statement is your friend.

Delisted/worthless stocks: No zakat on value that no longer exists. If a company went bankrupt and shares are $0, there’s nothing to pay on.

Stocks bought mid-year: Only zakatable after one lunar year (hawl) passes on them. Many investors simplify by using a single annual zakat date for everything.

Inherited stocks: The hawl starts when you take ownership, not when the deceased bought them.

Common Mistakes

  1. Using purchase price instead of market value — always market value on your zakat date
  2. Forgetting dividends sitting as cash in the brokerage account
  3. Skipping purification — thinking zakat covers it (it doesn’t)
  4. Double-counting DRIP dividends — they’re already in the share value
  5. Ignoring unvested vs vested — only count what you actually own

FAQs

Do I pay zakat on stocks I just bought?

Only after one lunar year (hawl) passes on them — same rule as all zakatable wealth. Most investors pick one annual zakat date (many use Ramadan) for simplicity.

Market value or purchase price?

Market value on your zakat date. Always. What you paid is irrelevant.

What about stock in a private company?

Estimate fair value (recent funding round valuation, book value, or a professional appraisal). If truly unknowable, scholars allow deferring zakat until sale — then pay for the missed years.

Do I pay zakat on my ESPP (employee stock purchase plan) shares?

Yes, once purchased and in your account — they’re your shares at market value.

What if my broker holds my dividends as cash?

It’s your cash — include it in your zakatable cash total.

Can I deduct margin loan balances?

If you owe a margin loan, the debt can be deducted from your zakatable assets (debts due within the year reduce net wealth) — but margin trading itself involves interest and is problematic independently.

I hold stocks in a Roth IRA — different rules?

No — the zakat calculation is identical. (Roth vs Traditional only affects taxes, not zakat.)
